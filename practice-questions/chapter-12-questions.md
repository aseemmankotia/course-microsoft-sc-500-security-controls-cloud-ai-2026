## Chapter 12: Full Practice Exam Simulation & Time-Management Strategy — Practice Questions

**Q1.** You start the exam with 120 minutes for 55 questions, including two case studies with 6 questions each. After 60 minutes you have completed both case studies and 20 standalone questions. What should you do next?

A) Go back and re-read both case studies in full to double-check every answer before continuing
B) Slow down significantly since you are ahead of schedule and can afford to overanalyze each question
C) Check your remaining time against remaining question count, then proceed at a pace that leaves 5-10 minutes for final review
D) Skip the remaining standalone questions and submit early to guarantee a passing score

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: C**

Continuously recalculating remaining time vs. remaining questions is the core time-budgeting skill — it keeps pace steady and preserves a buffer for flagged-question review.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Re-reading completed case studies wastes time that should go toward unanswered questions and final review
- Slowing down removes the review buffer and risks running out of time on later questions
- Skipping guaranteed points to submit early is never a valid strategy — unanswered questions are scored as wrong

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q2.** A case study has one long scenario followed by 5 questions of varying difficulty. What is the recommended reading strategy?

A) Read every word of the scenario carefully before looking at any question, then answer all 5 from memory
B) Answer questions using only the question text and never read the scenario, since most answers are self-contained
C) Skim the scenario once for structure and key facts, then re-read only the specific sections referenced by each question as you answer it
D) Read the scenario paragraph by paragraph, answering each question the moment relevant text appears

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: C**

Skim-then-targeted-reread is faster because it avoids memorizing details never tested and lets you locate the exact clause each question depends on.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Full careful reading up front wastes time on details that may not be tested and taxes memory unnecessarily
- Case study questions almost always depend on scenario-specific constraints (licensing, roles, existing config) — skipping the scenario causes wrong answers
- Answering mid-read causes you to miss later constraints that change the correct answer, forcing rework

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q3.** With 10 minutes left and 8 flagged questions still unanswered, you realize you cannot fully analyze all 8. What is the BEST approach?

A) Quickly eliminate obviously wrong options on each of the 8, select your best guess, and submit an answer for every one before time expires
B) Leave all 8 blank and use the time to recheck questions you already answered confidently
C) Spend the remaining time deeply analyzing only the 2 hardest flagged questions and leave the other 6 blank
D) Change several already-submitted answers based on a hunch that they might be wrong

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

Blank answers are always scored wrong, so a fast elimination-and-guess pass across all flagged items maximizes expected score under time pressure.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Leaving flagged questions blank guarantees zero credit on all 8 when partial credit via guessing is available
- Rechecking confident answers rarely changes the outcome and forfeits guaranteed guess-points on the flagged 8
- Deep analysis of only 2 questions abandons 6 guaranteed guessable points for marginal gain on 2

</details>

**Q4.** During review, you have used 100 of 120 minutes and have 20 minutes left with all questions answered. Your practice exam history shows your weakest domain is Domain C. What should you do with the remaining time?

A) Randomly re-check answers across all domains in the order they were originally answered
B) Spend equal time reviewing every domain regardless of past performance
C) Re-open and re-verify only the flagged questions belonging to Domain C first, then use any leftover time on other flagged items
D) Do nothing further since all questions already have an answer selected

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: C**

Prioritizing review time on your historically weakest domain maximizes the expected point gain per minute spent, since that's where error probability is highest.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Random order review ignores known weak-domain risk and wastes time on already-strong areas
- Equal-time review across domains is inefficient — weaker domains deserve proportionally more scrutiny
- Skipping review entirely forfeits a chance to catch errors when time is available

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q5.** You need to recommend a single service that provides centralized, cross-workload security alert correlation and long-term log retention for a customer with hybrid on-premises and multi-cloud resources. Which is the BEST answer under time pressure?

A) Microsoft Defender for Cloud alone
B) Microsoft Purview Audit
C) Microsoft Entra ID Protection
D) Microsoft Sentinel

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

Sentinel is the cloud-native SIEM built specifically for cross-workload correlation and long-term retention across hybrid and multi-cloud sources.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Defender for Cloud provides posture management and workload protection but is not a SIEM/correlation-and-retention platform on its own
- Purview Audit handles compliance/audit logging, not security alert correlation
- Entra ID Protection is scoped to identity risk detection only, not cross-workload correlation

</details>

**Q6.** A scenario question lists four remediation options for a compromised account. Two mention deprecated or non-existent portal actions, one is technically correct but doesn't fully address the described risk, and one fully resolves the scenario. What elimination approach finds the answer fastest?

A) Immediately eliminate any option referencing deprecated features or portals, then compare the two remaining options against every stated requirement in the scenario
B) Choose the first option that sounds technically correct without checking the others
C) Select the longest, most detailed-sounding option since detail usually indicates correctness
D) Eliminate the shortest answer choice as a rule of thumb

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

Removing clearly deprecated/incorrect options first narrows the field quickly, then comparing survivors against every scenario requirement catches the partial-solution distractor.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Picking the first plausible answer risks selecting the partial-fix distractor instead of the fully correct one
- Answer length is not correlated with correctness on this exam type and is a common false heuristic
- Answer brevity/length has no bearing on correctness and is not a valid elimination rule

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q7.** A question asks you to place four remediation steps in the correct order using a drag-and-drop interface. You are unsure of one step's exact position. What is the BEST approach?

A) Skip the item entirely since partial credit is never given on ordering questions
B) Place the steps you are confident about first, then use logical dependency reasoning (what must happen before what) to position the uncertain step, and flag it for review if time allows
C) Leave the entire list in its default, unordered state to avoid making things worse
D) Guess a full order randomly without applying any reasoning, since ordering questions are equally likely to be right regardless of logic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

Anchoring confident steps and using dependency logic for the uncertain one maximizes the chance of full or partial correctness, and flagging preserves a chance to revisit.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Skipping forfeits guaranteed partial or full credit that logical placement could earn
- Leaving the default order is effectively a guaranteed-wrong answer if the default isn't the correct sequence
- Random guessing ignores available dependency logic that meaningfully improves odds over chance

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q8.** What is the primary difference between 'skimming' and 'targeted re-reading' as case study reading strategies?

A) Skimming means reading only the question stems and ignoring the scenario completely
B) They are the same technique with different names
C) Targeted re-reading means reading the entire case study a second time from start to finish for every question
D) Skimming is a fast first pass to build a mental map of the scenario's key facts; targeted re-reading is a focused second pass that revisits only the specific passage relevant to the current question

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

Skimming builds overview awareness quickly; targeted re-reading is precise and question-driven, avoiding the time cost of full re-reads.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Skimming still means reading the scenario itself, just quickly — not skipping it in favor of question stems only
- The two techniques serve different purposes (broad overview vs. precise verification) and are not interchangeable
- Re-reading the entire case study per question is exactly the time-wasting behavior targeted re-reading is meant to avoid

</details>
